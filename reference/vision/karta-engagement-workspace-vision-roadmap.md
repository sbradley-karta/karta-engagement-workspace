# Karta Engagement Delivery Workspace

## Product Vision and Strategic Roadmap

**Status:** Vision and roadmap working draft  
**Date:** September 6, 2026  
**Strategy lead:** Sean Bradley  
**Planning horizon:** North-star direction beyond the first release

## Recommended Framing

The Engagement Delivery Workspace should become Karta's AI-enabled operating environment for implementation delivery. It should not be positioned as a collection of generators or a replacement for the systems teams already use. Its role is to connect project context, Karta methodology, approved AI capabilities, human judgment, and client-ready outputs in one governed engagement experience.

The larger vision is a workspace that helps delivery teams answer five questions throughout an engagement:

1. Where are we in the implementation lifecycle?
2. What needs attention now?
3. What work should happen next?
4. What evidence and prior decisions should inform that work?
5. What has been reviewed and is ready for client use?

The product should grow through connected delivery workflows, not isolated features. Each expansion should improve the quality or speed of a real project activity and should create structured, reviewed information that can support later phases.

## Vision Statement

Every Karta engagement has a trusted AI-enabled workspace that understands the project, guides the team through Karta's implementation methodology, accelerates high-value delivery work, preserves decisions and approved knowledge, and strengthens delivery confidence from sales handover through implementation, hypercare, and continuous support.

## Strategic Ambition

The product can become the practical expression of Karta's AI strategy within client delivery. It provides a common environment through which Karta can:

- Embed AI into normal delivery work instead of requiring separate prompting habits
- Apply Karta methodology, quality standards, templates, and brand consistently
- Connect sales context, meetings, requirements, designs, builds, tests, training, deployment, support, and decisions
- Reduce repeated interpretation and manual reassembly of project information
- Preserve engagement knowledge for onboarding, handoffs, and future support
- Develop junior team members through role-aware guidance, approved examples, and reusable delivery patterns
- Surface delivery risks and missing evidence earlier
- Measure where AI improves outcomes and where it creates review burden
- Scale approved capabilities without rebuilding access, provenance, review, and artifact controls for each use case

The long-term advantage is not simply faster drafting. It is a more connected and measurable delivery system in which project evidence becomes reviewed knowledge and reviewed knowledge becomes better downstream execution.

## The Problem the Product Solves

Implementation teams work across project plans, task systems, meeting notes, email, documents, models, test logs, training materials, and status decks. The information needed to make a good delivery decision often exists, but it is fragmented and has to be reconstructed repeatedly.

This creates several recurring forms of delivery friction:

- Client history, expectations, demonstrations, and commitments are lost between sales and delivery
- Project context is recreated in meetings and prompts
- Emerging solution designs are not consistently reconciled to contracted scope, timeline, and budget
- Requirements, designs, tests, and training materials drift apart
- Build activity is difficult to trace to requirements, and sprint feedback can become uncontrolled iteration
- UAT approaches vary without a deliberate operating model or comparable measures
- Training, closeout, hypercare, and support transitions are treated as secondary activities
- Project status requires manual collection and interpretation
- Decisions and assumptions become difficult to trace
- New team members depend on tribal knowledge
- Quality varies by individual experience and available time
- Delivery leaders receive risk signals late or without supporting evidence
- Useful AI experiments remain isolated because they do not share controls or data structures

The workspace should reduce this friction while keeping source systems authoritative and people accountable for client-facing work, commercial decisions, acceptance, and transition readiness.

## North-Star Experience

A team member enters through the engagement workspace and immediately sees the current lifecycle stage, its date range, any parallel workstreams, upcoming obligations, delivery exceptions, recent decisions, team availability, and recommended next actions. The workspace already knows the engagement, the user's role, the approved source locations, and the capabilities available for that project.

The user can move from orientation into a specific workflow without reconstructing context. For example:

- A project manager reviews current evidence, resolves source gaps, approves the weekly narrative, and produces the status deck.
- A delivery lead accepts a structured sales handover, understands the client relationship and what was demonstrated, and confirms the scope baseline before kickoff.
- A functional consultant turns workshop evidence into requirements, a design document, a process flow, and a mockup with traceability between them.
- A junior consultant receives role-specific guidance, approved examples, and relevant Karta patterns while still working within the project's review structure.
- A model builder sees approved requirements and design decisions, records build evidence, and identifies uncovered, changed, or repeatedly revised requirements.
- A test lead selects an appropriate UAT operating model, creates scenarios from approved requirements, tracks coverage and participation, and summarizes defects against business intent.
- A training lead manages a distinct workstream that may overlap Build, Test, and Deploy, producing the training outputs included in the engagement scope.
- A deployment and support lead manages cutover, formal closeout, optional hypercare, and a controlled transition to Karta's Continuous Support team.
- A delivery lead asks the project a question, sees the supporting evidence, and understands which risks or decisions require intervention.

The workspace remembers approved work and material decisions, but it does not replace Asana, SharePoint, Outlook, Granola, Harvest, Anaplan, or other authoritative systems.

## How Delivery Teams Could Use the Workspace

### At Engagement Start

- Review and accept a standard Sales Handover package
- Understand the client's business, relationship history, stakeholders, use case, and expected value
- Review what was proposed, demonstrated, discussed, and committed during the sales cycle
- Confirm the proposal, SOW, assumptions, exclusions, delivery window, budget, and immediate dependencies
- Use relevant Karta examples, guidance, and lessons learned to accelerate team readiness, particularly for junior resources

### Daily

- Review project insights and upcoming obligations
- Ask grounded questions about requirements, decisions, milestones, meetings, and prior outputs
- Continue work on a draft artifact without losing its evidence or review history
- Record a confirmed decision, action, assumption, or requirement
- Identify source gaps or conflicts before they affect downstream work

### After Workshops and Meetings

- Summarize outcomes and unresolved questions
- Propose actions, decisions, requirements, and risks for review
- Connect each accepted item to the originating meeting evidence
- Route accepted information into the requirements, decision, RAID, or workplan record
- Prepare follow-up communication for a person to review and send

### Weekly

- Review source freshness and milestone status
- Prepare and approve the weekly project narrative
- Generate the Karta status deck from approved values
- Review overdue work, aging RAID items, upcoming milestones, and PTO or capacity signals
- Confirm decisions and dependencies that need leadership attention

### At Phase Transitions

- Assess whether required deliverables and approvals are complete
- Reconcile the current design and delivery forecast to approved scope, schedule, budget, and capacity
- Identify gaps between requirements, design, build evidence, tests, and training
- Prepare a phase-readiness summary and transition checklist
- Package approved artifacts for the next delivery team
- Record accepted risks, deferred items, and ownership

### During Deployment and Support Transition

- Track production readiness, cutover, closeout obligations, and outstanding decisions
- Manage an engagement-specific hypercare period, typically four to six weeks when included
- Monitor stabilization measures, recurring issues, ownership, and readiness to exit hypercare
- Transfer approved project history, runbooks, known limitations, open items, and stakeholder context to Continuous Support
- Feed support findings and recurring patterns back into the delivery methodology and reusable Karta guidance

### During Team Changes

- Provide a role-specific onboarding brief
- Answer defined project questions with citations
- Explain current scope, status, decisions, and unresolved issues
- Direct the new team member to current approved artifacts
- Reduce dependence on a single person's memory

## Alignment to the Karta Delivery Lifecycle

The source implementation methodology uses five high-level phases. For the product vision, Karta should extend that model into seven lifecycle stages: Project Planning, Foundations, Build, Test, Train, Deploy, and Continuous Support. Train and Deploy are separated because they have different owners, deliverables, readiness criteria, and timing. Continuous Support is added because delivery responsibility and client value do not end at production release.

The journey should not imply that all work is strictly sequential. Train may begin during late Build and commonly overlaps Test. Deployment readiness may also run alongside Test and Train. Hypercare forms the controlled bridge between Deploy and Continuous Support. The workspace should therefore show a primary current stage and any active parallel workstreams.

### Project Planning

**Delivery objective:** Transfer commercial and client context into delivery, then establish a controlled engagement with clear scope, logistics, roles, sources, milestones, and working expectations.

Potential capabilities:

- Standard Sales Handover package and delivery-team acceptance
- Client company, relationship history, and account context
- Stakeholder map, influence, prior interactions, and known sensitivities
- Sales-cycle record of discovery, proposals, demonstrations, examples, and commitments
- Use-case framing, intended value, and Karta perspective based on relevant prior experience
- Proposal, SOW, assumptions, exclusions, budget, timeline, and scope baseline
- Reusable examples, delivery guidance, and role-aware onboarding for junior resources
- Engagement setup assistant
- Source and access validation
- Kickoff agenda and presentation support
- First-two-weeks meeting planner
- Stakeholder and responsibility mapping
- Initial delivery plan and milestone validation
- Data intake and first-draft coordination
- Engagement brief and onboarding package
- Early risk, assumption, and dependency identification

How teams use it:

The sales and delivery owners complete a standard handover and record delivery-team acceptance. The project manager then configures the workspace, confirms authorized sources, imports the lifecycle dates, validates the commercial and delivery baseline, and prepares the kickoff package. Team members begin with a shared understanding of the client relationship, use case, commitments, risks, and relevant Karta experience rather than separate local notes.

### Foundations

**Delivery objective:** Convert business intent and workshop evidence into an approved solution foundation, while confirming that the emerging design remains deliverable within the agreed scope, window, budget, and capacity.

Potential capabilities:

- Meeting Closeout
- Business Requirements drafting and quality review
- Requirements traceability and duplicate detection
- Design Document generation and consistency checks
- Process Benchmark synthesis
- Business Process Flow generation
- Mockup Generator
- List and Data Matrix drafting
- Data mapping and gap analysis
- Solution Architecture drafting
- Decision and assumption management
- Design-to-SOW and requirements-to-scope reconciliation
- Estimate, capacity, schedule, and budget impact assessment
- Classification of findings as in scope, clarification, trade-off, or change request
- Change-control trigger with client-ready options and impact framing

How teams use it:

Consultants move from workshop evidence to reviewed requirements, designs, process flows, and mockups. Each artifact retains its evidence and relationships. Before the solution baseline is approved, the workspace compares the design to the original commercial and delivery baseline. Any divergence is surfaced with options, delivery impact, ownership, and the required change-control decision rather than being carried silently into Build.

### Build

**Delivery objective:** Configure the solution through a disciplined, traceable build process and obtain documented initial business acceptance at each sprint review.

Potential capabilities:

- Project Build Plan creation
- Requirements-to-build coverage
- Data Hub design and mapping validation
- Model configuration documentation
- Dashboard and report specification support
- Security design validation
- Build evidence capture
- Configuration quality checks
- Design-deviation and change-impact summaries
- Sprint review preparation, evidence, feedback, and attendance record
- Initial business acceptance or explicit follow-up by reviewed item
- Iteration, rework, and change-request monitoring
- Development standards and build-quality checklists
- Future AI-assisted development review and quality assurance

How teams use it:

Every material build item traces to an approved requirement and design decision. Model builders record evidence against that lineage, and sprint reviews capture what was shown, business feedback, initial acceptance, follow-up actions, and newly requested changes. The workspace highlights missing coverage, design deviations, repeated revisions, and iteration beyond agreed expectations. Longer-term AI review can improve consistency and quality, but it should supplement defined Karta build standards and accountable peer review.

### Test

**Delivery objective:** Demonstrate that the solution meets approved business requirements through a deliberate UAT operating model and is ready for formal acceptance.

Potential capabilities:

- Test Plan drafting
- UAT approach design based on in-person, remote, facilitated, independent, or hybrid testing
- Participant, environment, access, schedule, support, and readiness planning
- UAT Test Script generation
- Requirements and risk-based coverage analysis
- Test data preparation support
- Defect classification and summarization
- Duplicate-defect detection
- Test and Enhancement Log management
- Retest and regression recommendations
- User sign-off evidence package
- Test-readiness and exit assessments
- Mode-appropriate participation, execution, defect, retest, and sign-off measures

How teams use it:

Test leads select the UAT delivery model before finalizing the plan. The workspace adapts scripts, participant instructions, facilitation support, evidence requirements, and measures to that model. AI should be used extensively to draft scenarios and scripts, assess coverage, classify defects, identify duplicates, recommend regression scope, and summarize progress. People remain accountable for business acceptance and sign-off.

### Train

**Delivery objective:** Prepare each in-scope audience to adopt, operate, administer, or teach the solution through a distinct workstream that can run in parallel with Build, Test, and Deploy.

Potential capabilities:

- Model Builder training documentation
- Administrative training and operational runbooks
- Train-the-trainer program and facilitator materials
- End-user training guides
- AI-supported training videos, facilitator scripts, and on-demand narration
- Role-based learning paths
- Training scope, audience, format, schedule, ownership, and completion plan
- Learning readiness, participation, completion, and feedback measures
- Content-to-design consistency checks

How teams use it:

The training lead confirms which deliverables are included in scope, such as administrator training, train-the-trainer, end-user training, training materials, AI-generated videos, or administrative runbooks. The workspace manages these as a separate plan, highlights dependencies on changing functionality, and prevents outdated design or test content from becoming approved training material.

### Deploy

**Delivery objective:** Move the solution into production, complete the implementation responsibly, and stabilize it through an optional engagement-specific hypercare period.

Potential capabilities:

- Cutover planning and readiness checks
- Release communication drafting
- Production validation and go-live decision package
- Deliverable, approval, documentation, and decision closure checklist
- Project closeout, lessons learned, and final client handoff
- Optional four-to-six-week hypercare plan, issue tracking, and stabilization measures
- Hypercare issue synthesis, recurring-pattern detection, and exit assessment
- Continuous Support transition readiness
- Anaplan roadmap and enhancement synthesis

How teams use it:

The deployment lead coordinates readiness, cutover, production validation, formal closeout, and open-item ownership. When hypercare is included, the workspace manages the defined four-to-six-week stabilization period and provides evidence for the exit decision. Hypercare should have explicit entry, service, measurement, ownership, and exit criteria rather than becoming an undefined extension of the project.

### Continuous Support

**Delivery objective:** Transfer operational ownership to Karta's dedicated Continuous Support team, sustain the solution, and turn recurring support evidence into improvements and roadmap decisions.

Potential capabilities:

- Structured support handover and receiving-team acceptance
- Approved runbooks, contacts, access, architecture, known limitations, and decision history
- Open issue, defect, enhancement, and deferred-scope transfer
- Service-level, severity, triage, escalation, and ownership guidance
- Incident, service request, defect, and enhancement classification
- Recurring-issue and root-cause analysis
- Adoption, usage, value, and solution-health monitoring where data is available
- Enhancement backlog, release planning, regression scope, and roadmap support
- Feedback of reusable lessons and quality patterns into Karta methodology and guidance

How teams use it:

The implementation and Continuous Support owners complete a controlled transition with named acceptance. The support team starts with the reviewed project history, stakeholder context, approved solution artifacts, operating procedures, open items, and known constraints. The workspace helps separate production incidents from service requests and enhancements, preserves traceability to the original intent, and turns recurring evidence into proactive recommendations for the client and Karta delivery teams.

## AI Capability Model

The workspace should support several levels of AI assistance. Autonomy should increase only when the risk, controls, and evidence justify it.

| Level | AI role | Example | Human control |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Retrieve | Find and organize authorized evidence | Locate the approved requirement and related decision | User confirms source relevance |
| 2 Synthesize | Summarize or structure evidence | Summarize workshop outcomes or open defects | User corrects and accepts the synthesis |
| 3 Draft | Produce an editable starting point | Draft a requirement, design section, test script, or training guide | Named review before client use |
| 4 Recommend | Identify options, gaps, or likely next actions | Recommend a status, test coverage, or phase-readiness concern | User owns the decision |
| 5 Prepare action | Assemble a proposed update or communication | Prepare a workplan change or client email draft | User reviews the exact action |
| 6 Execute controlled action | Perform an approved system action | Create a draft record or save an approved artifact | Explicit confirmation, logged execution, and constrained permissions |

The product should remain human-led. It may become more proactive, but it should not silently change plans, approve deliverables, communicate with clients, or make commercial commitments.

## Connected Delivery Chains

The roadmap should prioritize a small number of connected chains because they create compounding value.

### Chain 1: Sales-to-Delivery Continuity

Client and relationship context → sales-cycle evidence → use-case framing → proposal and SOW baseline → delivery-team acceptance → kickoff

This chain prevents the engagement from beginning with incomplete commercial or relationship context. It also creates the approved baseline against which later scope, timing, and budget decisions are assessed.

### Chain 2: Delivery Governance

Sources and milestones → Weekly Status → reviewed deck → decisions and RAID updates → next-period context

This is the first chain and establishes the recurring operating rhythm, approval model, deterministic artifact generation, and measurement approach.

### Chain 3: Solution Definition and Scope Alignment

Workshop evidence → Business Requirements → Design Document → Process Flows → Mockups → scope, schedule, and budget reconciliation → approved solution baseline or change decision

This chain reduces repeated interpretation during Foundations, creates the inputs required for build and test traceability, and prevents design choices from silently exceeding the commercial or delivery baseline.

### Chain 4: Traceable Build and Business Acceptance

Approved requirement and design → build item → build evidence → sprint review → business feedback → initial acceptance, follow-up, or change control

This chain creates the discipline Karta needs to manage build quality, coverage, iteration, and client expectations consistently.

### Chain 5: Validation

Approved requirements and design → UAT operating model → Test Plan → UAT Test Scripts → execution evidence → defects and retest → sign-off package

This chain strengthens coverage, adapts execution and measurement to the testing approach, connects failures to business intent, and supports defensible acceptance decisions.

### Chain 6: Adoption, Deployment, and Support

Approved process and solution → scoped training tracks → cutover package → production validation → project closeout → optional hypercare → Continuous Support acceptance → enhancement roadmap

This chain improves adoption, makes project closure explicit, and preserves operating knowledge after the implementation team transitions. It also creates a feedback loop from production evidence into future improvements and Karta delivery guidance.

## Product Architecture Direction

The future product should maintain a clear separation between:

### Engagement Experience

The user-facing workspace, methodology journey, screens, conversations, insights, and artifact workflows.

### Shared Foundations

Identity, engagement configuration, source access, provenance, review, approval, versioning, readiness, templates, document assembly, logging, and evaluation.

### Capability Services

The rules, retrieval plans, model prompts, deterministic logic, validation, review surfaces, and output contracts specific to each delivery capability.

### Engagement Record

The approved decisions, requirements, artifact versions, relationships, and review history needed for continuity and downstream reuse.

### Source Systems

The authoritative systems that hold project tasks, documents, communications, meeting evidence, time, and solution data.

The engagement record should grow from proven workflow needs. Karta should avoid designing a large enterprise data model before the connected capability chains demonstrate what information must be retained.

## Product Principles

1. Lead with the project, not the AI tool.
2. Use Karta methodology as the organizing model.
3. Resolve context before asking the user to work.
4. Show evidence before conclusions.
5. Keep source systems authoritative.
6. Use deterministic code for rules and artifact assembly.
7. Use AI where synthesis and judgment create value.
8. Make Unknown and Not assessed normal, visible states.
9. Require human accountability for client-facing work.
10. Connect outputs so value compounds across phases.
11. Release capabilities according to evidence and risk.
12. Measure corrections, trust, adoption, and delivery outcomes.
13. Carry relationship and commercial context from sales into delivery.
14. Reconcile solution decisions to scope, time, budget, and capacity before commitment.
15. Trace build, test, training, deployment, and support work to approved intent.
16. Treat business feedback, initial acceptance, formal sign-off, and change approval as distinct records.
17. Model parallel delivery work honestly rather than forcing a purely sequential journey.
18. Use AI and approved examples to accelerate junior-resource development without removing accountable review.
19. Treat hypercare and Continuous Support transition as planned delivery outcomes, not administrative afterthoughts.

## Risks and Trade-Offs

### Platform Scope Before Workflow Value

The vision can easily expand into a broad delivery platform. The roadmap should continue funding specific connected workflows with measurable outcomes rather than building generalized infrastructure without proven demand.

### Review Burden

Faster drafting is not valuable if consultants spend equal or greater time correcting output. Correction rate and review time should be measured at the field and capability level.

### False Confidence

Polished output can appear more certain than the evidence supports. Provenance, freshness, visible gaps, neutral status states, and named approval are product requirements, not optional governance layers.

### Knowledge Versus Source of Truth

The engagement record improves continuity but could create conflicting status or ownership. It should retain approved interpretations, relationships, and versions while continuing to point to authoritative source systems.

### Sales Context Versus Contracted Commitment

What was discussed or demonstrated during the sales cycle is important context, but it is not automatically contracted scope. The Sales Handover must distinguish client history, ideas, demonstrations, expectations, proposal language, and signed commitments so the workspace does not convert relationship context into unintended delivery obligations.

### Scope and Margin Protection

Foundations may reveal a solution that cannot be delivered within the original window, budget, or capacity. The product must make the divergence visible and support a timely decision. It should not make commercial judgments or allow a technically plausible design to bypass change control.

### Acceptance and Iteration Discipline

Sprint feedback can be mistaken for formal acceptance, while repeated preferences can be absorbed as ordinary iteration. The workspace should distinguish initial business acceptance, open follow-up, defect, clarification, design revision, and scope change. Governance must remain practical enough that teams use it consistently.

### Parallel Workstream Complexity

Training, testing, deployment readiness, and defect resolution may overlap. A purely linear interface can misrepresent readiness, while an overly complex dependency model can burden delivery teams. The product should show a primary stage, active parallel workstreams, key dependencies, and meaningful exceptions.

### AI-Assisted Quality Review

AI can help review build quality, test coverage, training consistency, and support patterns, but only after Karta defines the standards and evidence to evaluate. Model output should be advisory and should not be confused with peer review, business acceptance, or production approval.

### Hypercare and Support Ownership

Hypercare can become an undefined extension of implementation, and support teams can inherit incomplete context or unresolved project work. Entry, duration, measures, service expectations, owners, exit criteria, and receiving-team acceptance should be explicit for every applicable engagement.

### Engagement Isolation

The larger the retrieval and portfolio vision becomes, the greater the risk of cross-client exposure. Engagement filters must be enforced by services, not trusted to page configuration or model instructions.

### Capability Ownership

Capabilities will degrade if no one owns evaluation, defects, source changes, templates, and support. Ownership and readiness must be conditions for release.

### Client Access

Client access may create value later, but it changes expectations for security, reliability, support, retention, and accountability. It should remain a separately approved roadmap decision.

## Assumptions to Confirm

- The primary long-term users are Karta delivery teams, with client access considered later.
- The Karta delivery lifecycle for this product consists of Project Planning, Foundations, Build, Test, Train, Deploy, and Continuous Support.
- The source methodology remains the foundation, with Train and Deploy intentionally separated and Continuous Support added for the product vision.
- Training may run in parallel with late Build, Test, or Deploy rather than following a strictly sequential lifecycle.
- Hypercare is optional by engagement and, when included, is expected to last approximately four to six weeks unless the approved plan states otherwise.
- Karta's dedicated Continuous Support team may assume ownership after hypercare through a defined acceptance process.
- Sales-cycle information is valuable context but only approved contract, scope, assumption, and change records establish delivery commitments.
- The product will use one governed shell with engagement-specific configuration and isolation.
- Karta is prepared to establish product, foundation, and capability ownership as adoption grows.
- The initial connected lifecycle should prioritize requirements, design, mockups, and testing.
- Leadership will evaluate investment using delivery and trust measures, not generation volume alone.
- Portfolio and commercial capabilities will have stricter access controls than general project capabilities.
